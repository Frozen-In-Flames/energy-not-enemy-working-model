C-03: why the model's number and the book's number differ
==========================================================

The claim (SPEC.md, C-03): the reference home design day carries
220 kWh of beam energy on the aperture to 27.6 kWh of net
electricity with a passive condenser, about 92% of a 30 kWh/day
demand. That figure is the book's, from Energy Not Enemy.

What the model produces: the dynamic solver's continuous-operation
day reaches 24.2 kWh net, about 88% of the book figure. The
regression battery records claim C-03 as PARTIAL, an expected
difference, and it is deliberately not tuned away.

Why they differ: the two numbers are computed under different
assumptions, and both are honest under their own.

1. The book's balance is steady-state arithmetic. It prices the
   design day as if the plant were already at its operating point,
   with no warm-up cost.

2. The dynamic model starts cold. On the first morning it spends
   part of the incoming energy charging 180 kg of water and steel
   up to the design point before net production matches the
   steady-state rate. That warm-up energy is real, and the book's
   arithmetic does not charge for it.

3. The book's condenser is a fixed 40 C boundary. The dynamic
   model's passive condenser runs hotter than 40 C on a Texas
   afternoon, which lowers the cycle efficiency for those hours.

So the gap is not an error in either number. It is the measured
distance between a steady-state design figure and a dynamic
simulation of the same plant on the same design day. The battery
(tests/run_battery.py) prints both figures and the percentage on
every run, and results/claims-check.json carries the PARTIAL
verdict, so the difference stays visible instead of being smoothed
over.

What would close the gap: nothing in the model needs fixing. A
reader comparing the book to the model should read 27.6 kWh as the
steady-state design value and 24.2 kWh as the dynamic
continuous-operation value, with the cold-start and condenser
effects above accounting for the difference.
